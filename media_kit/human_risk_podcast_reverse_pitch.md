```markdown
On a recent episode of The Human Risk Podcast, Christian Hunt hosted Professor Alex Edmans to deconstruct why brilliant minds make catastrophic decisions in high-stakes environments.

Alex dropped an insight that every investor, CCO, and board member should tattoo on their forearm:

"More data is not always better. Investors often drown in quantitative data while completely ignoring the qualitative behavioral signals that actually hold predictive value."

He’s spot on. But take that dynamic out of public equities and drop it into M&A, private equity due diligence, or corporate governance—and the problem gets substantially worse.

In corporate diligence, we fall victim to the "IBM Syndrome": 
No one gets fired for commissioning a 60-page Big 4 audit checklist. It’s auditable. It checks the KYC boxes. It gives boards a clean CYA file.

Yet, here is a number that should bother every dealmaker:

👉 Standard due diligence catches only 33% of organizational risks.
👉 The remaining 67% of catastrophic failures stem from behavioral and implementation gaps.

The anti-bribery policy was in the data room.
The hotline SOP existed.
The code of conduct was signed.

None of that tells you whether anyone crosses the threshold of the CEO’s door.

In his work at Ethics Insight and his newsletter The Integrity Gap, Rupert Evill frequently compares board dashboards to Abraham Wald’s famous WWII bomber paradox.

During the war, the military mapped bullet holes on returning aircraft to reinforce the damaged areas. Wald stopped them: they were studying only the planes that survived. The fatal damage was in the unmarked areas of the planes that fell into the sea.

Boards do the exact same thing:
They audit clean compliance files and green dashboard metrics (the returning bombers). Meanwhile, they remain totally blind to the unrecorded behavioral friction that actually craters enterprise value:

1. The "Open Door" Myth:
When a founder boasts: "We have an open-door culture," don’t ask to see the policy. Ask: "Great. When was the last time someone actually walked through that door?" Who doesn't walk in tells you where the fatal hits are.

2. The Camera-Off Effect:
In a 5-person due diligence call, when a founder or fund manager turns their camera off the moment benign lending questions are asked, you don't need a 40-page financial model to understand how authority and psychological safety really flow in that business.

3. "Signal of Silence":
Thousands of frontline employees across high-risk jurisdictions with zero whistleblower reports isn't proof of integrity. It’s proof of fear, apathy, or lack of trust.

As Alex Edmans and Christian Hunt rightly pointed out, market madness doesn't happen because people lack intelligence. It happens because smart people cling to quantitative spreadsheets to justify decisions, while refusing to read the human room.

Curious to hear your take, Christian Hunt and Alex Edmans: In your experience, why are institutional boards so terrified of quantifying qualitative behavioral friction, even when they know the spreadsheets are lying to them?
```
